IPC / BNS LEGAL REFERENCE — SECTIONS AND PROCEDURES
(General legal reference for chatbot Q&A. This is a summary aid only — for a specific case, the authoritative Act/Section values always come from the live database's Act and Section tables, not this document. For operational reference; verify against the official text.)

IMPORTANT NOTE ON IPC vs BNS: India's Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) with effect from 1 July 2024. Cases registered before that date are recorded under IPC section numbers; cases registered on/after that date use BNS section numbers. When a user asks 'what is Section X' with no other qualifier, treat it as an IPC section number unless they say BNS or the case context indicates otherwise.

PART 1: IPC SECTIONS
==============================================================================

--- IPC SECTION 34 — Acts done by several persons in furtherance of common intention ---
Plain-language meaning: When several people share a common intention to commit a crime and one of them commits the criminal act, every one of them is held liable for that act as if they had each done it personally. It is not a standalone offence — it attaches to whatever offence was actually committed.
Punishment: Same as the substantive offence committed by the group
Cognizable: Depends on the underlying offence
Bailable: Depends on the underlying offence
BNS equivalent: 3(5)

--- IPC SECTION 120A — Definition of criminal conspiracy ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines criminal conspiracy as an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act, or a legal act by illegal means. This is a definition section — the punishment is in Section 120B.
Punishment: None (definition only — see 120B)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 61(1)

--- IPC SECTION 120B — Punishment for criminal conspiracy ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes the agreement itself to commit an illegal act, even if the act is never carried out. If the conspiracy is to commit an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or rigorous imprisonment of two years or more, it is punished the same as abetting that offence; conspiracies for lesser offences get a lighter punishment.
Punishment: Same as abetting the planned offence (for serious offences); otherwise up to 6 months, fine, or both
Cognizable: Depends on the offence conspired for
Bailable: Depends on the offence conspired for
BNS equivalent: 61(2)

--- IPC SECTION 109 — Punishment of abetment if the act abetted is committed ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes a person who abets (instigates, conspires, or intentionally aids) an offence, when that offence actually goes on to be committed as a result — with the same punishment as the offence itself, unless a specific abetment provision says otherwise.
Punishment: Same as the offence abetted
Cognizable: Depends on the offence abetted
Bailable: Depends on the offence abetted
BNS equivalent: 45

--- IPC SECTION 511 — Attempt to commit offences punishable with imprisonment for life or other imprisonment ---
Plain-language meaning: A general provision punishing an attempt to commit an offence (where no specific 'attempt' section exists for that offence), when the attempt itself does not succeed.
Punishment: Up to half the longest term provided for the completed offence, or as prescribed, plus/or fine
Cognizable: Depends on the offence attempted
Bailable: Depends on the offence attempted
BNS equivalent: 62

--- IPC SECTION 141 — Unlawful assembly ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines an unlawful assembly as five or more people gathered with a shared unlawful object, such as to commit an offence, resist the law, or forcibly take possession of property.
Punishment: None (definition — see 143)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 189(1)

--- IPC SECTION 143 — Punishment for unlawful assembly ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes membership in an unlawful assembly as defined in Section 141.
Punishment: Up to 6 months, or fine, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 189(2)

--- IPC SECTION 146 — Rioting ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines rioting as an unlawful assembly (or its members) using force or violence in pursuit of their shared unlawful object.
Punishment: None (definition — see 147)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 191(1)

--- IPC SECTION 147 — Punishment for rioting ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes participation in a riot as defined in Section 146.
Punishment: Up to 2 years, or fine, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 191(2)

--- IPC SECTION 148 — Rioting, armed with deadly weapon ---
Plain-language meaning: A more serious form of rioting where the rioter is armed with a deadly weapon or something capable of causing death.
Punishment: Up to 3 years, or fine, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 191(3)

--- IPC SECTION 149 — Unlawful assembly — every member guilty of offence committed in prosecution of common object ---
Plain-language meaning: If any member of an unlawful assembly commits an offence in pursuit of the group's shared object (or one the group knew was likely), every member of that assembly is guilty of that offence, not just the person who physically did it.
Punishment: Same as the offence committed
Cognizable: Depends on the offence committed
Bailable: Depends on the offence committed
BNS equivalent: 190

--- IPC SECTION 182 — False information, with intent to cause public servant to use his lawful power to the injury of another person ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes knowingly giving false information to a public servant that causes that official to act (or not act) in a way that unlawfully harms someone else — e.g. filing a false complaint to get an innocent person investigated.
Punishment: Up to 6 months, or fine up to ₹5,000, or both
Cognizable: No
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 217

--- IPC SECTION 186 — Obstructing public servant in discharge of public functions ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes voluntarily obstructing a public servant from carrying out their official duties.
Punishment: Up to 3 months, or fine up to ₹500, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 221

--- IPC SECTION 191 — Giving false evidence ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes making a false statement under oath (or under a legal obligation to state the truth) in a judicial proceeding.
Punishment: Up to 7 years and fine (up to life if used to secure conviction for a capital offence)
Cognizable: No
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 227

--- IPC SECTION 201 — Causing disappearance of evidence of offence, or giving false information to screen offender ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes destroying or hiding evidence of a crime, or giving false information, with the intention of protecting the real offender from legal punishment.
Punishment: Up to 7 years and fine if the offence is punishable with death; scaled down for lesser offences
Cognizable: Depends on the underlying offence concealed
Bailable: Depends on the underlying offence concealed
BNS equivalent: 238

--- IPC SECTION 212 — Harbouring an offender ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes knowingly sheltering or hiding a person to protect them from arrest, having reason to believe they committed an offence.
Punishment: Scaled to the offence harboured (up to 5 years for a capital offence)
Cognizable: Depends on the offence harboured
Bailable: Depends on the offence harboured
BNS equivalent: 249

--- IPC SECTION 299 — Culpable homicide ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines culpable homicide as causing death with the intention of causing death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with knowledge that the act is likely to cause death. This is a definition section — see 302/304 for punishment.
Punishment: None (definition only)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 100

--- IPC SECTION 300 — Murder ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines when culpable homicide amounts to murder — essentially, when the act is done with clear intention to kill or cause fatal injury, subject to specific exceptions (e.g. grave and sudden provocation, private defence). This is a definition section — see 302 for punishment.
Punishment: None (definition only)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 101

--- IPC SECTION 302 — Punishment for murder ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes the intentional killing of another person under circumstances that meet the legal definition of murder in Section 300, without any recognized exception applying.
Punishment: Death or life imprisonment, and shall also be liable to fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 103(1)

--- IPC SECTION 304 — Punishment for culpable homicide not amounting to murder ---
Plain-language meaning: Covers killing where the intention or knowledge required for murder is present, but a legal exception applies (e.g. sudden fight without premeditation) so it does not qualify as murder.
Punishment: Life imprisonment or up to 10 years and fine (if intent to kill/cause fatal injury); up to 10 years, fine, or both (if only knowledge, no intent)
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 105

--- IPC SECTION 304A — Causing death by negligence ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes causing death through a rash or negligent act that does not amount to culpable homicide — the classic example is a fatal road accident caused by careless driving.
Punishment: Up to 2 years, or fine, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 106(1)

--- IPC SECTION 304B — Dowry death ---
Plain-language meaning: Applies when a married woman dies from burns, bodily injury, or under otherwise abnormal circumstances within 7 years of her marriage, and it is shown she was subjected to cruelty or harassment for dowry shortly before her death.
Punishment: Minimum 7 years, extendable to life imprisonment
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 80(2)

--- IPC SECTION 306 — Abetment of suicide ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes intentionally instigating, aiding, or conspiring in an act that leads a person to take their own life.
Punishment: Up to 10 years and fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 108

--- IPC SECTION 307 — Attempt to murder ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes an act done with the intention or knowledge required for murder, in circumstances such that — had death actually resulted — the accused would have been guilty of murder, even though the victim survived.
Punishment: Up to 10 years and fine; life imprisonment if the victim is actually hurt by the act
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 109

--- IPC SECTION 308 — Attempt to commit culpable homicide ---
Plain-language meaning: The Section 307 equivalent for acts that would amount to culpable homicide not amounting to murder (rather than murder) had death resulted.
Punishment: Up to 7 years, or fine, or both; up to 3 additional years if hurt is caused
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 110

--- IPC SECTION 309 — Attempt to commit suicide ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes a person who attempts to take their own life and survives. In practice this section is now applied very rarely — the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 directs that a person who attempts suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and generally should not be prosecuted.
Punishment: Up to 1 year, or fine, or both
Cognizable: No
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: No general equivalent — BNS narrows this to Section 226 (attempting suicide specifically to compel or restrain a public servant from discharging duty); a plain attempt to suicide is not penalized under BNS

--- IPC SECTION 319 — Hurt ---
Plain-language meaning: Defines hurt as causing bodily pain, disease, or infirmity to any person. This is a definition section — see 323 for the general punishment.
Punishment: None (definition only)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 114

--- IPC SECTION 320 — Grievous hurt ---
Plain-language meaning: Lists specific categories of serious injury (e.g. emasculation, permanent loss of sight/hearing, fracture, disfiguration of the face/head, injury endangering life) that are classified as 'grievous' rather than simple hurt.
Punishment: None (definition only — see 325/326)
Cognizable: N/A (definition section)
Bailable: N/A (definition section)
BNS equivalent: 116

--- IPC SECTION 323 — Punishment for voluntarily causing hurt ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes intentionally causing bodily pain, disease, or infirmity to another person, where the injury doesn't rise to 'grievous hurt'.
Punishment: Up to 1 year, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both
Cognizable: No
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 115(2)

--- IPC SECTION 324 — Voluntarily causing hurt by dangerous weapons or means ---
Plain-language meaning: A more serious form of causing hurt, where the injury is inflicted using a weapon, fire, poison, or another dangerous means.
Punishment: Up to 3 years, or fine, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 118(1)

--- IPC SECTION 325 — Punishment for voluntarily causing grievous hurt ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes intentionally causing an injury that falls within the 'grievous hurt' categories defined in Section 320.
Punishment: Up to 7 years and fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes
BNS equivalent: 117(2)

--- IPC SECTION 326 — Voluntarily causing grievous hurt by dangerous weapons or means ---
Plain-language meaning: A more serious form of grievous hurt, inflicted using a weapon, fire, poison, or other dangerous means.
Punishment: Life imprisonment, or up to 10 years and fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 118(2)

--- IPC SECTION 326A — Voluntarily causing grievous hurt by use of acid ---
Plain-language meaning: Specifically punishes causing grievous hurt (including permanent disfigurement) by throwing or administering acid.
Punishment: Minimum 10 years, extendable to life imprisonment, and fine to be used for the victim's medical treatment
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 124(1)

--- IPC SECTION 326B — Voluntarily throwing or attempting to throw acid ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes the act of throwing, or attempting to throw, acid at a person, or attempting to administer it, even if it doesn't result in the injuries covered by 326A.
Punishment: Minimum 5 years, extendable to 7 years, and fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: 124(2)

--- IPC SECTION 336 — Act endangering life or personal safety of others ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes doing a rash or negligent act that endangers human life or the personal safety of others, even if no injury actually results.
Punishment: Up to 3 months, or fine up to ₹250, or both
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: Yes

--- IPC SECTION 337 — Causing hurt by act endangering life or personal safety of others ---
Plain-language meaning: Punishes causing hurt (not grievous) through a rash or negligent act that endangers life or personal safety.

Cognizable: Yes

--- IPC SECTION 338 — Causing grievous hurt by act endangering life or personal safety of others ---
Plain-language meaning: The grievous-hurt version of Section 337 — a rash or negligent act that endangers life and results in serious injury.
Punishment: Up to 2 years, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both

Bailable: Yes

--- IPC SECTION 354 — Assault or criminal force to woman with intent to outrage her modesty ---

--- IPC SECTION 354A — Sexual harassment ---
Plain-language meaning: Covers unwelcome physical contact and advances, demands for sexual favours, showing pornography against a woman's will, or making sexually coloured remarks.

--- IPC SECTION 354C — Voyeurism ---

--- IPC SECTION 354D — Stalking ---

--- IPC SECTION 377 — Unnatural offences ---
Plain-language meaning: Historically punished 'carnal intercourse against the order of nature'. Punishment: Up to 10 years, or life imprisonment, and fine
Cognizable: Yes
Bailable: No
BNS equivalent: No direct equivalent — BNS 2023 omitted this section; non-consensual acts of this nature are addressed via the general sexual-offence provisions

==============================================================================
PART 2: PROCEDURAL CONCEPTS
==============================================================================

--- CONCEPT: FIR (First Information Report) ---
An FIR is the written record a police station prepares when it first receives information about the commission of a cognizable offence (Section 154 CrPC / Section 173 BNSS). It sets the criminal investigation in motion — the informant's statement is recorded, read back to them, signed, and a copy is given to them free of cost. An FIR can only be registered for a cognizable offence; for a non-cognizable offence, the police record it in a different register (the NC/non-cognizable report) and cannot investigate without a magistrate's order.

--- CONCEPT: Zero FIR ---
A Zero FIR is an FIR registered by any police station, regardless of where the offence actually occurred or which station has jurisdiction over that area. It is given a temporary '0' serial number and is later transferred to the police station that does have jurisdiction, which then re-registers it under its own regular serial number. This exists so a victim is never turned away just because they walked into the 'wrong' police station — the police are legally required to register it and forward it, not redirect the complainant elsewhere first.

--- CONCEPT: Chargesheet ---
A chargesheet (formally the 'police report' under Section 173 CrPC / Section 193 BNSS) is the final investigation report the police file in court once they complete their investigation. It lists the evidence gathered, the accused persons, and the specific offences the police believe are made out. Filing a chargesheet is what moves a case from the investigation stage to the trial stage before a magistrate/court. If the investigation finds no case is made out, the police instead file a 'B Report' (closure report) rather than a chargesheet.

--- CONCEPT: Cognizable offence ---
A cognizable offence is one for which the police can arrest a person without a warrant and start an investigation without needing prior permission from a magistrate. These are generally the more serious offences (e.g. murder, rape, robbery). Whether a specific IPC/BNS section is cognizable or not is fixed by the First Schedule to the CrPC (now the BNSS) — it does not depend on the facts of a particular case.

--- CONCEPT: Non-cognizable offence ---
A non-cognizable offence is one for which the police cannot arrest without a warrant, and cannot investigate without a magistrate's permission. These are generally less serious offences (e.g. simple hurt, defamation). A complaint about a non-cognizable offence is recorded by the police in an NC register, but the complainant is directed to approach a magistrate directly if they want an investigation or prosecution to proceed.

--- CONCEPT: Difference between cognizable and non-cognizable offences ---
The core difference is police power to act on their own: for a cognizable offence, the police can arrest without a warrant and start investigating immediately on their own authority (an FIR is registered). For a non-cognizable offence, the police cannot arrest without a warrant and cannot investigate without a magistrate's order — they only record the complaint (as an NC entry) and the complainant must approach a magistrate for the case to move forward. Generally, more serious offences (murder, rape, robbery) are cognizable, and less serious ones (simple hurt, defamation) are non-cognizable — the classification for each section is fixed by the First Schedule to the CrPC/BNSS, not decided case by case.

--- CONCEPT: Bailable offence ---
A bailable offence is one where bail is a matter of right — if the accused is willing to furnish bail, the police officer or the court is legally bound to release them. These are generally less serious offences.

--- CONCEPT: Non-bailable offence ---
A non-bailable offence is one where bail is not a matter of right — it is granted or refused at the discretion of the court, based on factors like the severity of the offence, risk of the accused fleeing or tampering with evidence, and their criminal history. These are generally more serious offences.

--- CONCEPT: Anticipatory bail ---
Anticipatory bail (Section 438 CrPC / Section 482 BNSS) is bail sought in advance of arrest, by a person who has reason to believe they may be arrested for a non-bailable offence. If granted by the Sessions Court or High Court, it directs that the person be released on bail if and when they are actually arrested, rather than having to apply for bail only after arrest.

--- CONCEPT: Remand ---
Remand is a magistrate's order sending an arrested person into further custody while the investigation continues, because it cannot be completed within the initial 24-hour period the police are allowed to hold someone without judicial authorization. Remand can be 'police custody' (the accused stays with the police, generally for a limited window early in the investigation) or 'judicial custody' (the accused is sent to jail while the case continues).

--- CONCEPT: NBW (Non-Bailable Warrant) ---
An NBW is a warrant issued by a court directing the police to arrest a person and produce them before the court, without giving the person the option of release on bail at the point of arrest — bail can only be sought afterward, from the court. It's typically issued when a person fails to appear in court despite being summoned, or is deliberately evading appearance.

--- CONCEPT: Bailable warrant ---
A bailable warrant directs the police to arrest a person, but allows them to be released immediately on furnishing bail (as specified in the warrant) rather than being kept in custody until produced before the court. It is typically used for less serious cases of non-appearance than an NBW.

--- CONCEPT: Panchnama ---
A panchnama is a written record, prepared by the investigating officer in the presence of independent local witnesses ('panchas'), documenting what was observed or seized during an investigative act — most commonly a scene-of-crime inspection, a search, or a seizure of property/evidence. The panchas sign the document to independently attest that its contents accurately reflect what took place, which helps the evidence stand up to scrutiny in court.

--- CONCEPT: Panchanama witness ---
A 'panch' (plural: panchas) is an independent, disinterested local person the investigating officer brings in to witness and sign a panchnama — their role is to give the record credibility by confirming, from outside the police, that the seizure/inspection happened as documented.

--- CONCEPT: Inquest ---
An inquest (Section 174 CrPC / Section 194 BNSS) is the police procedure for recording the apparent cause of an unnatural, suicidal, or suspicious death — noting the condition of the body and any marks of injury — before the body is sent for post-mortem examination. It is distinct from a chargesheet; it simply documents the circumstances of the death for the record and to guide further investigation.

--- CONCEPT: Chargesheet types (A, B, C summary) ---
Police investigations typically close in one of a few standard ways: an 'A' final report (a genuine chargesheet — enough evidence found, the case is sent for trial), a 'B' final report (the case is found to be false/mistake of fact, and closed without trial), or a 'C' final report (the offence occurred but the offender could not be traced/identified, so the case is closed as undetected, though it can be reopened if new evidence emerges).

--- CONCEPT: Cognizance ---
A magistrate 'takes cognizance' of an offence when they formally apply their judicial mind to the facts of a case (usually on a police report or a private complaint) to decide whether to proceed further, such as issuing process against the accused. This is a distinct legal step from an FIR being registered or an investigation being conducted — it's the point where the matter moves into the court's own hands.

--- CONCEPT: Section 41 CrPC / arrest without warrant ---
Section 41 CrPC (now Section 35 BNSS) lays out the conditions under which a police officer may arrest a person without a warrant — generally for cognizable offences, or where there is credible information/reasonable suspicion the person committed one, subject to the officer recording their reasons in writing, particularly for offences punishable with up to 7 years.

--- CONCEPT: Complainant vs accused vs victim ---
The complainant is the person who reports the offence to the police (they may or may not be the victim themselves — e.g. a family member or witness can file the complaint). The victim is the person who actually suffered the harm from the offence. The accused is the person named or identified as having committed the offence; they remain 'accused' — not convicted — until a court finds them guilty at the end of a trial.
